# Irrigation Methods and Water Needs of Crops

Review status: general guide compiled for Smart Krishi from standard agronomy references.

## Which crops need more water
Crops differ greatly in their water needs. Rice, sugarcane, banana and coconut need a lot of water. Maize, groundnut, cotton, sunflower, wheat and most vegetables need a moderate amount. Ragi, jowar, bajra, red gram, bengal gram and horse gram need little water and are suited to rainfed farming and dry areas.

## Critical stages when water matters most
Every crop has stages when shortage of water causes the biggest loss of yield, and irrigation should be given at these stages first when water is limited. For cereals such as rice, maize and ragi, flowering and grain filling are critical. For groundnut, flowering, pegging and pod development are critical. For pulses, flowering and pod filling are critical. For wheat, crown root initiation and flowering are critical. For vegetables, flowering and fruit development are critical.

## Drip irrigation
Drip irrigation delivers water drop by drop at the root zone through pipes and emitters. It saves a large share of water compared with flooding, reduces weeds because only the root zone is wet, and lets fertilizer be given with the water, which is called fertigation. It suits vegetables, sugarcane, banana, coconut, arecanut, mango and other orchards. Filters must be cleaned regularly so the emitters do not block. Subsidy for drip and sprinkler systems is available under the Per Drop More Crop part of PMKSY.

## Sprinkler irrigation
Sprinklers spray water over the crop like rain. They suit groundnut, pulses, vegetables, wheat and fodder on light and uneven soils, and save water compared with flooding. Avoid sprinkling in strong wind and in the hot afternoon, when much of the water evaporates.

## Furrow, basin and flood irrigation
In furrow irrigation, water flows in channels between the crop rows; it suits maize, cotton, sugarcane and vegetables. Basin irrigation, a ring of soil around each tree, suits orchard and plantation crops. Flooding the whole field wastes water and washes away nutrients, except in rice, which is grown in standing water.

## Saving water in the field
Mulching the soil with crop residues, dry leaves or plastic mulch reduces evaporation and weeds. Irrigate in the early morning or evening. Level the field so water spreads evenly. Farm ponds collect rain water for protective irrigation when the rains fail; the Krishi Bhagya scheme of Karnataka supports farm ponds. Check the soil moisture with the Smart Krishi sensor and by hand before every irrigation.

## Watering by soil type
Sandy soil holds little water, so it needs light and frequent irrigation. Clay and black soil hold much more water, so they need less frequent but heavier irrigation, and they must not be over-watered because they drain slowly. Red loamy soil is in between.
